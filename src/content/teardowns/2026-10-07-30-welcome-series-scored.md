A welcome email is the one message a subscriber is guaranteed to expect. They asked for it a few seconds ago. That makes it the cheapest chance a brand gets to set the terms of the relationship, and most brands spend it on a logo, a discount code, and a grid of bestsellers.

We signed up for 30 brands across eight categories and scored the first email each one sent. The pattern was consistent enough to state plainly: the welcome emails that scored highest did one thing the others did not. They asked the subscriber something, and said what they would do with the answer.

## What the top scores had in common

**They set expectations.** The Morning Ledger told new readers when the email would arrive, how long it would take to read, and what would be in it. That is three sentences, and it is the reason the email scored 5 on copy and voice.

**They asked for one preference.** Brightside Pet asked for the pet's type, age, and size, then explained that the answers would change which products showed up. It was the only welcome in the set to score 5 on personalization.

**They offered one next step.** Ledgerly's welcome had one button, with the number of minutes the step would take printed beside it.

## Where the rest lost points

The middle of the pack was not bad. It was generic. Subject lines like "Welcome to Cinder Kitchen" do their job and nothing more, and a functional welcome scores in the high 20s on this rubric because the rubric rewards specifics.

Two welcome emails in the full set would have been capped: one with a broken unsubscribe link and one with an expired discount code in the hero. Both are failures a pre-send check would catch.

## What to steal

1. Put the cadence in the preheader.
2. Ask one question and say why you are asking.
3. Give one next step with an honest time estimate.
